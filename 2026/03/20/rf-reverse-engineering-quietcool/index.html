Reverse Engineering the TabCat V2 Cat Tracker RF Protocol

March 20, 2026 · Brian Cohn · RF · SDR · Reverse Engineering

Motivation

My cat Kit is an indoor/outdoor explorer. After one too many anxious evenings wondering which neighbor's yard she'd chosen for the night, I picked up a TabCat V2 tracker—a directional RF homing device that clips to the collar and lets you triangulate your cat's position using a handheld receiver.

It works surprisingly well. But as someone who builds embedded hardware, I couldn't leave it at that. What frequency does it use? What's the protocol? What chip is inside? Could I build a custom receiver? This post documents my attempt to answer those questions using FCC filings, a PCB teardown, SDR analysis, and a lot of squinting at datasheets.

Step 1: The FCC Filing Deep Dive

Every wireless device sold in the US must have an FCC ID. The TabCat V2 has two—one for the handheld receiver and one for the tag that goes on the cat:

ComponentFCC IDFrequencyGranted
Handset (receiver)TUW-PH2435.0 MHz2022-07-20
Homing TagTUW-BT2435.0 MHz2022-07-21

Key finding: 2435 MHz

The TabCat operates at 2435 MHz—right in the 2.4 GHz ISM band, but on a proprietary protocol. It's not Bluetooth, not Zigbee, not WiFi. The FCC filing classifies it under "low power transmitters using spread spectrum techniques" (Part 15, Subpart C). The manufacturer is Loc8tor Ltd out of London, and the test reports were done by UL International UK.

Unfortunately, the block diagram, schematics, BOM, and operational description are all marked confidential in the filing—so the FCC documents give us frequency and regulatory data but nothing about the actual protocol. Time to open the device.

Full FCC test report: fcc.report/FCC-ID/TUW-BT

Step 2: PCB Teardown

I carefully opened the tag's enclosure. The PCB is compact—about the size of a thumbnail—with a single main IC, a crystal oscillator, chip antenna pads, and battery contacts for what appears to be a coin cell arrangement.

The Chip: Silicon Labs EFR32xG22

The markings on the IC read: 

G22
C224HG
C01K92
2132

After considerable searching, I'm fairly confident this is a Silicon Labs EFR32xG22C224 series chip—specifically the EFR32FG22 (proprietary wireless) variant. Here's my reasoning:

  • "G22" — matches the Silicon Labs Wireless Gecko Series 2 "xG22" family designation
  • "C224" — directly matches the ordering code suffix for the 2.4 GHz radio configuration
  • "2132" — consistent with a YYWW date code: week 32 of 2021
  • "C01K92" — likely a lot/trace code

EFR32FG22C224 Key Specs

  • ARM Cortex-M33 @ 76.8 MHz
  • 512 KB flash, 32 KB RAM
  • 2.4 GHz radio, TX up to +6 dBm
  • RX sensitivity: -102.3 dBm
  • QFN32 package (4×4 mm)
  • 1.8V–3.8V supply

The FG22 variant is designed specifically for proprietary wireless protocols—which aligns perfectly with TabCat's non-standard RF approach. The BG22 (Bluetooth) and MG22 (Zigbee/Thread) variants use the same silicon but different firmware stacks.

However, I want to be upfront: I couldn't find this exact marking pattern documented anywhere online. Silicon Labs' public ordering guides don't show "C224HG" as a suffix, and searching for the full marking string yields nothing. If you recognize this package marking convention or have experience with Silicon Labs chip identification, I'd love to hear from you.

Step 3: RF Signal Capture & Analysis

With the frequency identified at 2435 MHz, I set up an SDR capture. Note that a standard RTL-SDR tops out around 1.7 GHz, so capturing at 2.4 GHz requires either a HackRF One, a downconverter, or a specialized 2.4 GHz receiver. I used a wideband SDR with a 2 MHz sample rate centered on 2435 MHz.

The capture approach:

# Key parameters from FCC filing
CENTER_FREQ = 2_435_000_000   # 2435 MHz - from FCC ID TUW-BT
SAMPLE_RATE = 2_000_000       # 2 MHz bandwidth
GAIN        = 40              # dB

# Configure SDR
sdr.sample_rate = SAMPLE_RATE
sdr.center_freq = CENTER_FREQ
sdr.gain = GAIN

# Read IQ samples and compute amplitude envelope
samples = sdr.read_samples(1024)
amplitude = np.abs(samples)
peak = np.max(amplitude)

# Burst detection: proprietary protocol sends short pings
if peak > THRESHOLD:
    log_burst(timestamp, peak, duration)

The Mystery Signals

This is where I got stuck.

Near the end of my capture session, I observed two faint bursts at t≈4.8s and t≈5.2s. They're just barely above the noise floor. The spectral shape is different from both the handset beacon and the tag response:

  • Much shorter duration (~20–40ms vs ~80–120ms for normal bursts)
  • Lower amplitude (barely above detection threshold)
  • Slightly different center frequency offset
  • No corresponding beacon ping preceding them

Are these: (a) environmental 2.4 GHz interference (WiFi, Bluetooth, microwave oven)? (b) A different mode of the TabCat protocol—maybe an unprompted "heartbeat" beacon from the tag? (c) Multipath reflections of earlier transmissions? (d) Just noise that happened to cross the threshold?

I spent a while trying to correlate these with button presses, proximity changes, and other events, but couldn't nail down a pattern. The 2.4 GHz ISM band is notoriously crowded, so (a) is plausible. But the timing feels too coincidental—they appeared within the same session as the legitimate handset-tag exchanges.

The Chip Identification Dead End

Beyond identifying the likely chip family (EFR32xG22), I hit a wall. The specific marking C224HG doesn't appear in any Silicon Labs public documentation I could find. The "HG" suffix might encode flash size, RAM, or package variant in a shorthand that Silicon Labs uses on the physical marking but doesn't publish in ordering guides.

I also couldn't find any open-source firmware, protocol analysis, or reverse engineering work on the TabCat protocol specifically. Loc8tor Ltd appears to use a fully proprietary stack running on the FG22's radio, which makes sense—the directional homing functionality likely requires custom signal processing that wouldn't fit neatly into a standard BLE or Zigbee framework.

What I Learned

  1. FCC filings are invaluable. Even when the interesting documents are confidential, you get the exact operating frequency, test lab, and regulatory classification.
  2. 2.4 GHz proprietary protocols are hard to crack. Unlike 433 MHz OOK remotes (which rtl_433 eats for breakfast), proprietary 2.4 GHz protocols with GFSK modulation and custom framing require much more work to decode.
  3. Silicon Labs chip markings are opaque. Unlike some manufacturers who print full part numbers, SiLabs uses abbreviated markings that aren't straightforward to decode without reference samples.
  4. The 2.4 GHz band is noisy. Isolating a low-power proprietary signal from WiFi, Bluetooth, and other interference is non-trivial. A shielded enclosure or directional antenna would help.

Next Steps

  • Capture with a directional antenna pointed at the tag, to improve SNR and reduce ambient 2.4 GHz noise
  • Try GNU Radio with a GFSK demodulator tuned to 2435 MHz to extract the bitstream
  • Compare captures at different distances to see if the tag adjusts power or encoding
  • Look into the Silicon Labs RAIL (Radio Abstraction Interface Layer) API for clues about the protocol framing
