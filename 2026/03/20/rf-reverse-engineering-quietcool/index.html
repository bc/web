Reverse Engineering the TabCat V2 Cat Tracker RF Protocol

March 20, 2026 RF SDR Reverse Engineering Embedded

Motivation

My cat Kit is an indoor/outdoor explorer. After one too many anxious evenings wondering which neighbor's yard she'd chosen for the night, I picked up a TabCat V2 tracker—a directional RF homing device that clips to the collar and lets you triangulate your cat's position using a handheld receiver.

It works surprisingly well. But as someone who builds embedded hardware, I couldn't leave it at that. What frequency does it use? What's the protocol? What chip is inside? Could I build a custom receiver? This post documents my attempt to answer those questions using FCC filings, a PCB teardown, SDR analysis, and a lot of squinting at datasheets.

Step 1: The FCC Filing Deep Dive

Every wireless device sold in the US must have an FCC ID. The TabCat V2 has two—one for the handheld receiver and one for the tag that goes on the cat:

ComponentFCC IDFrequencyGranted
Handset (receiver)TUW-PH2435.0 MHz2022-07-20
Homing TagTUW-BT2435.0 MHz2022-07-21

Key finding: 2435 MHz via proprietary spread spectrum

The TabCat operates at 2435 MHz—right in the 2.4 GHz ISM band, but on a proprietary protocol. It's not Bluetooth, not Zigbee, not WiFi. The FCC filing classifies it under "low power transmitters using spread spectrum techniques" (Part 15, Subpart C, scope A4: UNII/spread spectrum). This is a meaningful detail—it tells us the modulation is likely OQPSK DSSS or frequency-hopping, not simple narrowband FSK. The manufacturer is Loc8tor Ltd out of London, and the test reports were done by UL International UK.

Unfortunately, the block diagram, schematics, BOM, and operational description are all marked confidential in the filing—so the FCC documents give us frequency and regulatory data but nothing about the actual protocol. Time to open the device.

Full FCC test report: fcc.report/FCC-ID/TUW-BT

Step 2: PCB Teardown

I carefully opened the tag's enclosure. The PCB is compact—about the size of a thumbnail—with a single main IC, a crystal oscillator, chip antenna pads, and battery contacts for what appears to be a coin cell arrangement.

The Chip: Silicon Labs EFR32FG22

The markings on the IC read:

G22
C224HG
C01K92
2132

After considerable searching, I'm fairly confident this is a Silicon Labs EFR32FG22C224—the proprietary wireless variant of their Wireless Gecko Series 2 platform. Here's my reasoning:

  • "G22" — matches the xG22 family designation (EFR32xG22)
  • "C224" — the ordering code suffix for the 2.4 GHz radio configuration
  • "2132" — YYWW date code: week 32 of 2021
  • "C01K92" — likely a lot/trace code

Does the chip match the signal? Yes.

I verified the EFR32FG22 datasheet against what the FCC filing and our capture show. The chip supports exactly the modulation types we'd expect:

  • GFSK at up to 2 Mbps—the wider-bandwidth beacon signals we see are consistent with high-rate GFSK
  • OQPSK DSSS at 250 kbps with -102.3 dBm sensitivity—this is the "spread spectrum technique" referenced in the FCC classification, and would explain the direction-finding capability (DSSS enables precise time-of-arrival measurements)
  • RFSense with selective OOK for ultra-low-power wake-up—the mystery bursts could be RFSense wake-up packets

The FG22 variant is specifically designed for proprietary protocols only—no Bluetooth, no Zigbee stack. It runs custom firmware via Silicon Labs' RAIL (Radio Abstraction Interface Layer). TX output up to +6 dBm, ARM Cortex-M33 at 76.8 MHz, 512 KB flash, 32 KB RAM, QFN32 (4×4 mm), 1.8–3.8V supply.

However, I want to be upfront: I couldn't find this exact marking pattern documented anywhere online. Silicon Labs' public ordering guides don't list "C224HG" as a suffix, and searching for the full marking string yields nothing. The "HG" likely encodes flash size, RAM, or package variant in an abbreviated form that Silicon Labs uses on the physical die marking but doesn't publish. If you recognize this package marking convention, I'd love to hear from you.

Step 3: RF Signal Capture & Analysis

With the frequency identified at 2435 MHz, I set up an SDR capture. A standard RTL-SDR tops out around 1.7 GHz, so capturing at 2.4 GHz requires either a HackRF One, a downconverter, or a specialized 2.4 GHz receiver. I used a wideband SDR with a 2 MHz sample rate centered on 2435 MHz. 

# Key parameters from FCC filing TUW-BT
CENTER_FREQ = 2_435_000_000   # 2435 MHz
SAMPLE_RATE = 2_000_000       # 2 MHz BW — captures full signal
GAIN        = 40              # dB

# Configure SDR
sdr.sample_rate = SAMPLE_RATE
sdr.center_freq = CENTER_FREQ
sdr.gain = GAIN

# Read IQ samples and compute amplitude envelope
samples = sdr.read_samples(1024)
amplitude = np.abs(samples)
peak = np.max(amplitude)

# Burst detection
if peak > THRESHOLD:
    log_burst(timestamp, peak, duration)

The Mystery Signals

This is where I got stuck.

Near the end of my capture, I observed two faint bursts at t≈4.8s and t≈5.2s. They sit just barely above the noise floor, and their shape differs from both the handset beacon and the tag response:

  • Much shorter duration (~20–40ms vs ~80–120ms for normal bursts)
  • Lower amplitude (barely above detection threshold)
  • Slightly different center frequency offset
  • No corresponding beacon ping preceding them

One intriguing possibility: the EFR32FG22 has an RFSense selective OOK wake-up mode. The tag could be emitting periodic low-power OOK wake-up beacons independently of the handset—a "heartbeat" so the handset can detect the tag's presence before starting the full ping-response sequence. The short duration and low power would be consistent with this.

But it could also be 2.4 GHz environmental interference (WiFi, Bluetooth, microwave). The band is crowded. I couldn't nail down a pattern linking these to button presses or proximity changes.

The Chip Identification Dead End

Beyond identifying the chip family, I hit a wall. The specific marking C224HG doesn't appear in any Silicon Labs public documentation. The "HG" suffix might encode flash size, RAM, or package variant in an abbreviated form not published in ordering guides.

I also found no open-source firmware, protocol analysis, or reverse engineering work on the TabCat protocol. Loc8tor uses a fully proprietary stack built on Silicon Labs' RAIL API, which makes sense—the directional homing functionality likely requires custom signal processing (RSSI gradient mapping, possibly phase-difference analysis) that wouldn't fit into a standard BLE or Zigbee framework.

What I Learned

  1. FCC filings are invaluable. Even when the interesting documents are confidential, you get the exact operating frequency, regulatory classification, and (critically) the modulation family—"spread spectrum" told us to expect DSSS/FHSS, not simple narrowband.
  2. Chip identification confirms the story. The EFR32FG22's OQPSK DSSS and GFSK capabilities perfectly match the FCC classification and the signal characteristics we observed. When the chip, the filing, and the capture all agree, you have high confidence in the analysis.
  3. 2.4 GHz proprietary protocols are hard to crack. Unlike 433 MHz OOK remotes (which rtl_433 eats for breakfast), proprietary 2.4 GHz with GFSK/DSSS and custom framing require specialized demodulation.
  4. The 2.4 GHz band is noisy. Isolating a low-power proprietary signal from WiFi, Bluetooth, and other interference is non-trivial. A shielded enclosure or directional antenna would help enormously.
